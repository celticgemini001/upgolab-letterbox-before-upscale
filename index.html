Letterboxed Video Before Upscaling: Crop the Bars First, and Prove It

Every "upscale to 4K" button assumes the frame is the picture. On a letterboxed or pillarboxed source it is not: a 1920×1080 frame can hold a 1440×1080 picture, which means a quarter of the pixels you are about to interpolate are pure black and are not part of any picture at all. The obvious move is to crop first. The interesting part is that the number your quality metric reports will not change if you do not — and the number that does change is a lot worse than the usual "it's only the edges" dismissal.

Measured 18 November 2026 · Pillow 12.2.0, numpy 1.26.4, ffmpeg 6.1.1 · known-answer masters generated at 3840×2880 with a seeded scene, then framed and re-measured

What "bars" actually cost you, in pixels

Take a 4:3 picture and put it in a 16:9 frame. The picture is 1440×1080; the frame is 1920×1080; so 240 pixels on the left and 240 on the right are not picture. That is 480 × 1080 = 518,400 pixels out of 2,073,600 — exactly 25.00%.

Upscale the frame as-is to 4K and you interpolate 8,294,400 output pixels. Upscale the cropped picture and fill the width yourself, and you interpolate 6,220,800 — the 25% is gone. The compute saving is not a rounding detail, and it is measurable:

PathPixels resampledTime, 4× lanczosWhat the resampler sees at the picture edge
Crop bars, then upscale6,220,800443 msThe edge of the picture
Upscale full frame, then crop8,294,400604 msThe edge of the picture plus 240 px of black
Difference−25.0%1.37× slower—

The 1.37× is not the 1.33× you would predict from area alone — measured on the same machine, same kernel, three runs each. The extra is fixed overhead that does not scale with pixel count, so the saving is real but slightly less than the area ratio suggests. If you are doing this to video, 604 ms against 443 ms per frame is 18.1 s against 13.3 s of pure resampling for one second of 30 fps footage — per second, for every second you process.

Experiment 1 — how deep does the black actually bleed?

Here is where the intuition is usually wrong in both directions. People who crop say "otherwise you get a soft edge"; people who do not crop say "it's only a pixel or two, nobody sees it". Both are half right, and the measured answer explains why.

The test scene deliberately puts maximum contrast at x=0 — a horizontal gradient that starts at luma 200 on the very first column and falls to 40 — so any leakage from the black bar has the largest possible signal to contaminate. Then: frame it, upscale the frame, crop the bars back out, and compare against cropping first and upscaling. Difference is reported in source pixels so the numbers are comparable across scale factors.

Two things fall out of this table that are not obvious from the "soft edge" story.

The depth is a kernel property, not a scale property. Lanczos contaminates 3.5 source pixels regardless of whether you scale 4× or 8×; bicubic contaminates 2.5 at 4× and 2.25 at 8×. This is exactly right: the support of the filter is fixed in source coordinates, so a wider kernel reaches further into the bar no matter how far out you sample. Doubling the output resolution doubles the number of affected output columns without changing how much of the picture is wrong.

The damage is severe exactly where it happens. PSNR in the first two output pixels is 11–18 dB. For scale, 30 dB is "visible but tolerable" and 40 dB is "hard to find". At 14 dB the first pixel of your picture is, numerically, not the picture — it is a blend of the picture and a black bar. Nobody would ship that if it were the whole frame. It is the whole frame only 0.2% of the time.

Experiment 2 — the metric cannot see it, and that is the real trap

Now the part that matters if you have a quality gate in CI. Run the two paths against the true master — not against each other — and report whole-frame PSNR:

KernelCrop-first, whole frameUpscale-first, whole frameΔCrop-first, first 8 pxUpscale-first, first 8 px
lanczos20.2920.28−0.0170.9832.98
bicubic20.3720.38+0.0170.9834.48

The wrong path is ahead by 0.01 dB on one kernel and behind by 0.01 dB on the other. That is noise — the number is identical to the precision anyone would report. Meanwhile the actual edge is 38 dB worse. A regression test built on whole-frame PSNR would pass the broken pipeline forever, and would pass it slightly more comfortably with bicubic.

This is not a subtle numerical artefact, it is the arithmetic of averaging. The contaminated region is 8 columns out of 2880, or 0.21% of the frame, at 33 dB; the other 99.79% sits at 71 dB. The global figure is dominated by the good part:

If the bad strip is…at 33 dB…at 30 dB
0.21% of the frame (8 of 3840 cols)59.5 dB global56.0 dB global
1% of the frame52.9 dB global50.0 dB global
5% of the frame46.0 dB global44.0 dB global

Read the budget backwards and it gets worse. Starting from a 45 dB baseline, a strip at 33 dB can cover 1.74% of the frame before the global number drops a single dB. At 30 dB it is 0.85%. A crop-edge defect is 5–8× smaller than the budget, so it will never show up in a whole-frame gate — no matter how you tune the threshold. The defect has to be measured where it lives, or not at all.

Practical consequence. If you want a gate that catches this, the gate has to be a strip metric: PSNR of the first 8 output pixels of the active area, against a reference. That number goes from 71 dB to 33 dB between the two paths. The whole-frame number goes from 20.29 to 20.28.

Experiment 3 — why cropdetect's default limit is 24, and when it breaks

The practical question is not "should I crop" — it is "will the crop be right". ffmpeg's cropdetect finds bars by scanning for rows and columns whose maximum luma is below limit, default 24. That default is not arbitrary, and the measurement shows exactly what it is worth.

Vary the bar's luma (pure black through luma 32, i.e. visibly grey) and add ±2 or ±4 luma of noise, which is what a real encode gives you. Record the detected active width at five limits:

The threshold test is max >= limit, so a bar whose brightest pixel is exactly the limit counts as picture and the crop fails silently — you get the full 1920 width back and no warning. Bar luma 24 with no noise defeats the default exactly. With ±4 noise it takes a bar at luma 20 to defeat it. This is why "just use the default" works in demos and fails on the disc rip that has a deliberately non-black matt: the bar is the same luma as the limiter.

The reverse failure is worse because it destroys picture. At limit=48 every bar in the test is detected, but the detector also clips a horizontal strip off the top — 1080 lines down to 996, 1014 or 1016 depending on the input — because a dark region inside the picture (the gradient's dark corner) is below 48 too. Raising the limit to catch a light bar costs you picture content. There is no limit that is both aggressive enough for a luma-32 bar and safe for dark image content; the rule has to be verified per source, not set globally.

Reproducing it

Everything above runs in about a second except the 4× timings. Pillow only; no ffmpeg needed for the metrics. The scene is deterministic.

import numpy as np
from PIL import Image, ImageDraw

def scene(W, H, edge_lit):
    yy, xx = np.mgrid[0:H, 0:W]
    a = np.zeros((H, W, 3), np.float32)
    if edge_lit:                      # max contrast exactly at x=0
        v = np.linspace(200, 40, W, dtype=np.float32)
        a[..., 0] = a[..., 1] = a[..., 2] = v[None, :]
    else:
        a[..., 0] = 40 + 60 * (xx / W)
        a[..., 1] = 44 + 50 * (yy / H)
        a[..., 2] = 52 + 40 * ((xx + yy) / (W + H))
    img = Image.fromarray(a.clip(0, 255).astype(np.uint8))
    d = ImageDraw.Draw(img)
    r = int(H * 0.2778)
    d.ellipse([W//2-r, H//2-r, W//2+r, H//2+r], fill=(220,210,190),
              outline=(255,255,255), width=max(2, H//360))
    return img

def psnr(a, b, sl=None):
    if sl: a, b = a[sl], b[sl]
    m = np.mean((a.astype(np.float64) - b.astype(np.float64)) ** 2)
    return float('inf') if m == 0 else round(10 * np.log10(255*255 / m), 2)

src = scene(1440, 1080, True)
frame = Image.new('RGB', (1920, 1080), (0, 0, 0))
frame.paste(src, (240, 0))                     # 240 px bar each side

for s in (2, 3, 4, 8):
    for kn, k in (('lanczos', Image.LANCZOS), ('bicubic', Image.BICUBIC)):
        A = np.asarray(src.resize((1440*s, 1080*s), k), np.uint8)
        B = np.asarray(frame.resize((1920*s, 1080*s), k), np.uint8)
        B = B[:, 240*s:240*s + 1440*s]         # crop the bars back out
        d = np.abs(B.astype(int) - A.astype(int)).max(axis=2)
        cols = int((d > 1).sum(axis=0).astype(bool).sum())
        print(f"{kn} {s}x  cols={cols:3d}  src_px={cols/s:4.2f}  "
              f"edge2={psnr(B, A, (slice(None), slice(0, 2))):6.2f}  "
              f"edge8={psnr(B, A, (slice(None), slice(0, 8))):6.2f}  "
              f"whole={psnr(B, A):6.2f}")

For the cropdetect table, the same frame is rebuilt at different bar luma and noise, then:

for lim in 16 24 32 40 48; do
  ffmpeg -v info -loop 1 -t 0.4 -i framed.png \
    -vf "cropdetect=limit=$lim:round=2" -f null - 2>>&1 \
    | grep -o 'crop=[0-9:]*' | tail -1
done

Note the -loop 1 -t 0.4: a single still through -frames:v 1 produces no crop= line at all on ffmpeg 6.1.1, because cropdetect logs from the second frame onwards. That is fifteen minutes of confusion the first time you script it.

What to actually do

  1. Crop before you resample, always. 25% fewer pixels, 1.37× faster, and the edge of your picture never sees a bar. There is no quality argument for upscaling black.
  2. Verify the detected rectangle instead of trusting it. Print the width cropdetect returns and compare against the width you expect from the container's display aspect ratio. If they differ, the crop is wrong and every subsequent number is measuring the wrong rectangle.
  3. Do not set limit to catch a light bar. At 48 the detector started eating picture content — 1080 lines became 996. Pick the limit from the source's actual bar luma, measured on one frame, not from a guess.
  4. Gate on an edge strip, not the whole frame. 8 output pixels at the active boundary catches a 38 dB defect that a whole-frame PSNR gate reports as 0.01 dB.
  5. Check the axis twice. Pillarboxing and letterboxing have different failure modes only in which scan direction you measure; the crop rectangle is the same operation, but a source can have bars on both axes and cropdetect will happily return a rectangle that is right in one and wrong in the other.

If you are doing this as part of a pipeline rather than by hand — crop, upscale, encode — the same order applies: the upscaler never needs to know the bars existed. The upgolab.com studio takes the cropped picture and does not try to be clever about frame geometry, which is the behaviour you want here.
